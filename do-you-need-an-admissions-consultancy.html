Independent preparation

Do you need an admissions consultancy?

How to organise an application, find free resources and prepare independently, with a brief look at when extra help might be useful.

By Arij Asad, Cambridge Mathematics graduate, former Cambridge interviewer and STEP examiner

For applications in autumn 2026 for entry in 2027, or deferred entry in 2028.

Start with the official guidanceFor most students, a general admissions agency is not necessary. The universities publish their requirements and provide free preparation resources. A clear plan, regular practice and feedback from school can take you a long way.

What the universities say

Cambridge states that commercial interview services are not endorsed by the university and do not have exclusive information. Oxford's Mathematics department says applicants should not feel they need special admissions training. It recommends practising mathematics and explaining your thinking.

My suggestion is to begin with those resources and your school's advice. You can then decide whether there is a particular part of the process you need help with.

How to organise your application

  1. Choose the course. Read what you would study and check the required subjects, grades and admissions tests. The course guides bring these together and link to the official information.
  2. Make a calendar. Include your school's deadlines, UCAS, test booking, access-arrangement requests and any additional university or college forms. Submitting UCAS does not register you for an admissions test.
  3. Plan regular practice. Keep up with school mathematics and add a few unfamiliar problems. Leave time to review your attempts and revisit ideas you found difficult.
  4. Explore your interests. Read about an idea, work through a proof or investigate a small problem. Keep notes on what you learned and what you would like to understand next.
  5. Write your application. Use examples you can explain in your own words. A teacher can help you spot unclear passages and suggest where more detail would be useful.
  6. Practise discussing problems. Explain an approach to a teacher, friend or family member. A partner who knows the subject can also ask follow-up questions.

Parents can help with deadlines, quiet study time and encouragement. Asking what a student learned from a difficult question is often more useful than focusing only on the score.

Free resources to start with

  • UAT-UK's official materials: TMUA and ESAT specifications, sample questions and computer-based practice.
  • Cambridge's STEP Support Programme: assignments with hints and solutions, progressing towards STEP 2 and STEP 3.
  • Oxford Maths interview guidance and Cambridge interview preparation: explanations and examples of the interview process.
  • The site's paper archives and preparation guides, topic practice and challenge problems.

Choose a small set of resources and use them regularly. Your school may also know about university outreach programmes you are eligible for.

When to start

In Year 12, start with regular problem solving alongside schoolwork. One substantial problem, followed by time to review it, can be a useful weekly routine. Over the summer and in early Year 13, add preparation for the test your course requires. The STEP timeline and TMUA plan give more detail.

Keep some official papers unseen until you are ready for timed practice. Once you remember the solutions, a paper tells you less about how you would handle new questions. Earlier in your preparation, selected questions and topic work can help you build the skills you need.

When extra help may be useful

If the same difficulty keeps coming up, feedback from a teacher or tutor can help you understand it. For example, you might need help setting up equations, writing a complete proof or explaining your reasoning. The next step should be something you can practise independently.

Questions students and parents ask

Can I apply without paid preparation?

Yes. Paid preparation is not an entry requirement. The universities provide free guidance, and the resources above give you a starting point for independent work.

Where can I find out what interviews assess?

Start with the university's interview guidance and any instructions from your college. Official examples show how a discussion develops and what interviewers are looking for.

Do I need a paid summer school or work experience?

Neither is a general requirement for the courses covered here. Reading, problems and small projects can help you explore the subject. Check your course's requirements before planning an activity around an application.

Can someone help with my personal statement?

A teacher or another reader can point out unclear explanations or repetition. The experiences and reflections should be your own. Use the three UCAS questions to organise what you want to say.
