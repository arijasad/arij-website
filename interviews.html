Five problems to discuss

Try a focused set of interview-style problems, with hints and commentary to support reflection.
A manageable preparation routine
  1. Choose a few unfamiliar problems rather than trying to finish every collection.
  2. Make a sustained attempt before opening a hint. Explain a useful example, diagram or possible approach aloud.
  3. Listen carefully to guidance, then try to develop the next step yourself.
  4. Afterwards, identify the idea you missed and return to a related problem. Include graph sketching and arguments built from simple cases.

Practice with a teacher, friend or study partner can be valuable.

Problem-solving resources
  • Cambridge interview preparation

    Start with the advice, practice questions and demonstration interviews. Use the commentary to think about how a discussion develops.

  • Mr Bowler’s problem-solving booklet

    A substantial collection of university interview-style problems. Choose a small set, attempt them independently, then discuss the reasoning.

  • STEP Support Programme

    Foundation assignments help build the algebra, reasoning and persistence needed for longer problems. Select assignments around a specific weakness.

  • Advanced Problems in Mathematics

    Free book with challenging STEP-based problems, commentary and solutions. Its older examination references are historical.

  • Oxford Online Maths Club

    Recorded mathematical discussions and worksheets. Pause before a solution and work through the problem yourself.

  • CSAT sample papers

    Optional mathematical and computing problems for extra practice. CSAT is not a Cambridge Mathematics admissions requirement.

  • BMO past problems

    Optional proof-based extension. Begin with accessible BMO1 questions and practise writing a complete argument.

  • Historical MAT papers

    Older long questions can support interview problem solving. MAT is no longer the Oxford admissions test for the 2027-entry round.

What makes a useful practice discussion?

Explain the mathematical decision that matters: what you notice, why an approach seems promising and where the uncertainty lies. Give yourself time to think, and treat a hint as information you can use.
